Lossy vs Lossless Image Compression Explained

By the Teknawy team · November 13, 2026 · 5 min read

Every image compressor uses one of two approaches: lossless compression, which keeps every pixel exactly the same, or lossy compression, which throws away some information to make the file much smaller. Understanding the difference helps you pick the right format and settings.

What is lossless compression?

Lossless compression finds patterns and repetition in the data and stores them more efficiently — similar to how a ZIP file works. When the image is opened, it is reconstructed perfectly. Nothing is lost.

Imagine a row of 100 identical blue pixels. Instead of storing “blue” 100 times, a lossless method stores “100 × blue”. That works brilliantly for graphics with large areas of flat colour, but photos contain subtle variations in almost every pixel, so there is little repetition to exploit.

Formats: PNG, GIF, lossless WEBP.

What is lossy compression?

Lossy compression goes further: it removes details that the human visual system is not very sensitive to. Our eyes notice changes in brightness much more than small changes in colour, and we notice large shapes more than fine high-frequency texture. JPEG takes advantage of this by storing colour at lower resolution and simplifying fine detail in small blocks of the image.

The quality setting controls how much is discarded. At high quality the result is visually identical to the original; at low quality you see blocky patches and blurred edges.

Formats: JPEG, lossy WEBP, AVIF.

Which should you use?

  • Use lossy compression (JPEG or WEBP at 75–85%) for photographs you are publishing or sharing.
  • Use lossless compression (PNG) for graphics with text and sharp edges, and for master copies you will edit again.
  • Keep your original files. Compress a copy for each use rather than repeatedly re-saving the same JPEG.

Why the quality slider does not affect PNG

Because PNG is lossless, there is no quality setting to lower. To make a PNG smaller you can reduce its dimensions, or convert it to a lossy format such as WEBP — see the PNG to WEBP converter.
