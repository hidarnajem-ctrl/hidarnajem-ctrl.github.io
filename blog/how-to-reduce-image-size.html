How to Reduce Image File Size Without Losing Quality

By the Teknawy team · November 13, 2026 · 6 min read

Large images are the most common reason a web page feels slow, an email bounces or an upload form refuses your file. The good news is that most photos can be made several times smaller without anyone noticing a difference. Here are the five techniques that matter most, in order of impact.

1. Resize to the size you actually need

A modern phone camera takes photos around 4000×3000 pixels — 12 million pixels. A typical blog column displays images about 800–1200 pixels wide. If you upload the full-resolution photo, the browser downloads millions of pixels that are thrown away when the image is scaled down on screen.

Because file size grows with the number of pixels, halving the width and height cuts the pixel count by about 75%. Resizing is almost always the single biggest saving. Use the image resizer and choose a maximum width such as 1920px for full-width banners or 1280px for article images.

2. Choose the right format

Each format is designed for a different kind of image:

  • JPEG — photographs, with excellent compatibility.
  • PNG — graphics with sharp edges, text and transparency. Very large for photos.
  • WEBP — both photos and graphics, with transparency, usually 25–35% smaller than JPEG.

A photo accidentally saved as PNG can easily be 5–10 times larger than the same photo as JPEG or WEBP. Converting it is a quick win — try the PNG to WEBP converter. For a deeper comparison, read JPG vs PNG vs WEBP.

3. Lower the quality setting — a little

Lossy formats such as JPEG and WEBP have a quality setting from 0 to 100. The relationship between quality and file size is not linear: going from 100 to 85 often halves the file, while the visual difference is tiny. Going from 85 to 75 saves a bit more. Below 60 you start to see blocky artefacts, especially around text and sharp edges.

Rule of thumb: start at 80%. Zoom in to 100% and look at edges, skies and skin. If it looks fine, try 70%. If you see artefacts, go back up.

4. Remove hidden metadata

Photos from cameras and phones contain EXIF metadata: camera model, lens, settings, date and sometimes GPS coordinates. This usually adds only a few kilobytes, but it can reveal where a photo was taken. Re-encoding an image in the browser, as Teknawy does, produces a clean file without this metadata.

5. Process images in batches

If you publish many images, consistency matters as much as individual savings. Pick one set of rules — for example “WEBP, 80% quality, max 1600px wide” — and apply it to every image. A batch tool lets you do this in one step and download everything as a ZIP.
